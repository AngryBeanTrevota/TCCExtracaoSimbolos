Sketch of a new theory of the human soul

Four or five gentlemen were debating, one night, several questions of high transcendence, without the disparity of votes bringing the slightest alteration to their spirits. The house was on Santa Teresa hill; the room was small, lit by candles, whose light mysteriously merged with the moonlight coming from outside. Between the city, with its commotions and adventures, and the sky, where the stars winked, through a clear and tranquil atmosphere, were our four or five investigators of metaphysical things, amicably solving the most arduous problems of the universe.

Why four or five? Strictly speaking, there were four who spoke; but besides them, there was a fifth person in the room, silent, thinking, dozing, whose contribution to the debate was no more than an occasional grunt of approval. This man was the same age as his companions, between forty and fifty years old, provincial, capitalist, intelligent, not without education, and, it seemed, astute and caustic. He never argued; and he defended himself from abstention with a paradox, saying that discussion is the polished form of the combatative instinct that lies in man, as a bestial inheritance; and he added that the seraphim and the cherubim did not dispute anything, and, besides, they were spiritual and eternal perfection. Since he gave this very answer that night, one of those present contradicted him and challenged him to prove what he said, if he could. Jacobina (that was his name) reflected for a moment, and answered:

- Thinking it over, perhaps you are right.

Suddenly, in the middle of the night, this stubborn man took the floor, and not for two or three minutes, but for thirty or forty. The conversation, in its meanderings, came to fall upon the nature of the soul, a point that radically divided the four friends.

Every head, every sentence; not only agreement, but discussion itself, became difficult, if not impossible, because of the multiplicity of questions that were deduced from the main trunk, and a little, perhaps, because of the inconsistency of the opinions. One of the arguers asked Jacobina for some opinion — a conjecture, at least.

- Neither conjecture nor opinion - he replied -; either one may give rise to dissent, and, as you know, I do not argue. But, if you want to hear me in silence, I can tell you a case from my life, in which the clearest demonstration on the matter under discussion stands out. First of all, there is not just one soul, there are two...

- Two?

- Nothing less than two souls. Every human creature carries two souls with him: one that looks from within outward, another that looks from without inward... Be as astonished as you like; you may stand there with your mouth open, shrug your shoulders, anything; I do not admit reply. If you reply to me, I finish my cigar and go to sleep. The outer soul may be a spirit, a fluid, a man, many men, an object, an operation. There are cases, for example, in which a simple shirt button is a person’s outer soul; and so too the polka, vinte e um, a book, a machine, a pair of boots, a cavatina, a drum, etc. It is clear that the office of this second soul is to transmit life, like the first; the two complete man, who is, metaphysically speaking, an orange. Whoever loses one of the halves naturally loses half of existence; and there are cases, not rare ones, in which the loss of the outer soul entails the loss of the whole existence.

Shylock, for example. That Jew’s outer soul was his ducats; to lose them was equivalent to dying.

“I shall never see my gold again,” he says to Tubal; “it is a dagger you bury in my heart.” Note this phrase well; the loss of the ducats, outer soul, was death for him. Now, it is necessary to know that the outer soul is not always the same...

- Not?

- No, sir; it changes in nature and state. I am not referring to certain all-absorbing souls, such as the homeland, with which Camões said he died, and power, which was Caesar’s and Cromwell’s outer soul. These are energetic and exclusive souls; but there are others, though energetic, of changeable nature. There are gentlemen, for example, whose outer soul, in the first years, was a rattle or a hobbyhorse, and later a brotherhood stewardship, let us suppose. For my part, I know a lady — indeed, a most charming one — who changes outer soul five, six times a year. During the lyric season it is the opera; when the season ends, the outer soul is replaced by another: a concert, a ball at the Cassino, Ouvidor Street, Petrópolis...

- Excuse me; who is this lady?

- This lady is the devil’s kinswoman, and has the same name: she is called Legion... And there are many other such cases. I myself have experienced such exchanges. I do not relate them, because it would take too long; I restrict myself to the episode I mentioned to you. An episode from my twenty-five years...

The four companions, anxious to hear the promised case, forgot the controversy. Holy curiosity! You are not only the soul of civilization, you are also the apple of concord, divine fruit, of a flavor other than that apple of mythology. The room, until a little while before noisy with physics and metaphysics, is now a dead sea; all eyes are on Jacobina, who adjusts the end of his cigar, gathering his memories. Here is how he began the narration:

- I was twenty-five years old, poor, and had just been appointed ensign in the National Guard. You cannot imagine what an event this was in our house. My mother was so proud! So happy! She called me her ensign. Cousins and uncles, it was all sincere and pure joy. In the village, mark this well, there were some offended people; weeping and gnashing of teeth, as in Scripture; and the reason was none other than that the post had many candidates and that they lost. I suppose also that part of the resentment was entirely gratuitous: it arose from mere distinction. I remember some young men who used to be friendly with me and began to look askance at me for some time. In compensation, I had many people who were pleased with the appointment; and proof of that is that all my uniform was given to me by friends... Then one of my aunts, D. Marcolina, widow of Captain Peçanha, who lived many leagues away from the village, in a secluded and solitary place, wished to see me, and asked that I go to her and bring the uniform. I went, accompanied by a page, who returned to the village some days later, because Aunt Marcolina, as soon as she had me at the place, wrote to my mother saying that she would not let me go before a month, at least. And she embraced me! She too called me her ensign. She found me a handsome fellow. Being somewhat jolly, she even came to confess that she was envious of the young woman who was to be my wife. She swore that in the whole province there was no one else who could outdo me. And always ensign; it was ensign here, ensign there, ensign at every hour. I asked her to call me Joãozinho, as before; and she shook her head, declaring no, that it was “Mr. Ensign.” A brother-in-law of hers, brother of the late Peçanha, who lived there, did not call me anything else. It was “Mr. Ensign,” not as a joke, but seriously, and in front of the slaves, who naturally followed the same path. At the table I had the best place, and was served first. You cannot imagine. If I tell you that Aunt Marcolina’s enthusiasm went so far as to have a large mirror placed in my room, a rich and magnificent work, which contrasted with the rest of the house, whose furnishings were modest and simple... It was a mirror given to her by her godmother, and that one had inherited it from her mother, who had bought it from one of the noblewomen who came in 1808 with the court of D. João VI. I do not know what there was of truth in this; it was tradition. The mirror was naturally very old; but one could still see the gold, partly eaten away by time, some dolphins carved in the upper corners of the frame, some mother-of-pearl ornaments and other artistic whims. All old, but good...

- Large mirror?

- Large. And it was, as I say, an enormous kindness, because the mirror was in the parlor; it was the best piece in the house. But there were no forces that could move her from her purpose; she replied that it was not needed, that it was only for a few weeks, and finally that “Mr. Ensign” deserved much more. The fact is that all those things, affection, attentions, courtesies, wrought in me a transformation, which the natural feeling of youth helped and completed. You imagine it, I think?

- No.

- The ensign eliminated the man. For some days the two natures were balanced; but it was not long before the original one yielded to the other; there remained in me a minimal part of humanity. Then it happened that the outer soul, which before was the sun, the air, the field, the eyes of the young women, changed in nature, and became the courtesy and the flatteries of the house, everything that spoke to me of the rank, nothing that spoke to me of the man. The only part of the citizen that remained with me was that which had to do with the exercise of the commission; the other dispersed into the air and into the past. Hard to believe, isn’t it?

- Hard even to understand - replied one of the listeners.

- You will understand. Facts will explain feelings better; facts are everything. The best definition of love is not worth a kiss from a girl in love; and, if I remember correctly, an ancient philosopher demonstrated motion by walking. Let us go to the facts. Let us see how, at the time when the man’s conscience was becoming obliterated, that of the ensign became alive and intense. Human pains, human joys, if they were only that, scarcely obtained from me an apathetic compassion or a favoring smile. At the end of three weeks, I was another, entirely another. I was exclusively an ensign. Now, one day Aunt Marcolina received grave news; one of her daughters, married to a farmer living five leagues away, was ill and near death. Farewell, nephew! Farewell, ensign! Being a devoted mother, she immediately arranged a trip, asked her brother-in-law to go with her, and told me to take care of the estate. I believe that, if it had not been for the distress, she would have arranged the opposite; she would have left the brother-in-law and gone with me. But the fact is that I was left alone, with the few slaves in the house. I confess to you that from the very start I felt a great oppression, something like the effect of four walls of a prison suddenly raised around me. It was the outer soul that was shrinking; it was now limited to a few uncouth spirits. The ensign continued to dominate in me, although life was less intense, and consciousness more feeble. The slaves put a note of humility into their courtesies, which in a certain way compensated for the affection of relatives and the interrupted domestic intimacy. I even noticed, that night, that they redoubled their respect, their joy, their protestations. Nhô ensign at every minute. Nhô ensign is very handsome; nhô ensign is to be a colonel; nhô ensign is to marry a pretty girl, daughter of a general; a concert of praises and prophecies that left me ecstatic. Ah! Perfidious ones! I could scarcely suspect the secret intention of the wicked.

- To kill you?

- Better if it were so.

- Something worse?

- Listen to me. The next morning I found myself alone. The scoundrels, seduced by others or of their own motion, had decided to flee during the night; and so they did. I found myself alone, with no one else, between four walls, facing the deserted yard and the abandoned plantation. No human breath. I ran through the whole house, the slave quarters, everything, nothing, no one, not even a little black boy. Only cocks and hens, a pair of mules, which philosophized on life, shaking off the flies, and three oxen. The dogs themselves had been taken by the slaves. No human being. Do you think this was better than dying? It was worse. Not from fear; I swear to you I was not afraid; I was a little bold, so much so that I felt nothing during the first hours. I was sad because of the harm done to Aunt Marcolina; I was also a little perplexed, not knowing whether I should go to her to give her the sad news, or remain in charge of the house. I adopted the second course, so as not to abandon the house, and because, if my sick cousin was badly off, I would only increase the mother’s pain, with no remedy whatsoever; finally, I expected that Uncle Peçanha’s brother would return that day or the next, since they had been gone for already thirty-six hours. But morning passed without a trace of him; and in the afternoon I began to feel a sensation like that of a person who had lost all nervous action and had no awareness of muscular action. Uncle Peçanha’s brother did not return that day, nor the next, nor throughout that whole week. My solitude took on enormous proportions. Never were the days longer, never did the sun scorch the earth with more tiring obstinacy. The hours struck from century to century, in the old clock in the room, whose pendulum, tick-tack, tick-tack, struck my inner soul like a continuous flick of eternity. When, many years later, I read an American poem, I believe by Longfellow, and came upon this famous refrain:

Never, for ever! - For ever, never!

I confess to you that I shuddered: I remembered those dreadful days. That was exactly how Aunt Marcolina’s clock went: - Never, for ever! - For ever, never! They were not swings of the pendulum, they were a dialogue of the abyss, a whisper of nothingness. And then at night! Not that night was more silent. The silence was the same as by day. But night was the shadow, it was solitude even narrower or wider. Tick-tack, tick-tack. No one in the rooms, on the veranda, in the corridors, in the yard, no one anywhere... Are you laughing?

- Yes, it seems he was a little afraid.

- Oh! It would have been good if I could have been afraid! I would have lived. But the characteristic of that situation is that I could not even be afraid, that is, in the ordinary sense of fear. I had an inexplicable sensation. It was like a dead man walking, a sleepwalker, a mechanical doll. Sleeping, it was another matter. Sleep gave me relief, not for the common reason of being the brother of death, but for another one. I think I can explain this phenomenon as follows: sleep, by eliminating the need for an outer soul, allowed the inner soul to act. In dreams, I dressed myself, proudly, in the midst of family and friends, who praised my bearing, who called me ensign; a friend of our house would come, and promise me the rank of lieutenant, another, that of captain or major; and all this made me live. But when I awoke, in broad daylight, the consciousness of my new and only self faded away with sleep — because the inner soul lost exclusive action, and remained dependent on the other, which stubbornly refused to return... It did not return. I went out, one way and another, to see if I could discover some sign of return.

Soeur Anne, soeur Anne, ne vois-tu rien venir? Nothing, nothing at all; just as in the French legend. Nothing but the dust of the road and the grass on the hills. I went back home, nervous, desperate, and threw myself on the parlor canapé. Tick-tack, tick-tack. I got up, walked about, drummed on the windowpanes, whistled. On one occasion I remembered writing something, a political article, a novel, an ode; I did not choose anything definitively; I sat down and wrote on the paper some scattered words and phrases, to intersperse in the style. But the style, like Aunt Marcolina, remained as it was. Soeur Anne, soeur Anne... Nothing at all. At most I saw the ink darken and the paper whiten.

- But you did not eat?

- I ate badly, fruits, flour, preserves, some roots toasted over the fire, but I would have borne everything cheerfully, if it had not been for the terrible moral situation in which I found myself. I recited verses, speeches, Latin passages, Gonzaga’s lyrics, Camões’s octaves, décimas, an anthology in thirty volumes. At times I did gymnastics; at others I pinched my legs; but the effect was only a physical sensation of pain or tiredness, and nothing more. All silence, a vast, enormous, infinite silence, only underlined by the eternal tick-tack of the pendulum. Tick-tack, tick-tack...

- Indeed, it was enough to drive one mad.

- You are going to hear something worse. It is worth telling you that, since I had been left alone, I had not looked once at the mirror. It was not deliberate abstention, I had no reason; it was an unconscious impulse, a fear of finding myself one and two, at the same time, in that solitary house; and if such an explanation is true, nothing better proves the human contradiction, because at the end of eight days, I got the whim to look at the mirror precisely in order to find myself two. I looked and drew back. The glass itself seemed conspired with the rest of the universe; it did not stamp my figure clearly and whole, but vague, blurred, diffuse, shadow of a shadow. The reality of physical laws does not permit denying that the mirror reproduced me exactly, with the same contours and features; so it must have been. But that was not my sensation. Then I became afraid; I attributed the phenomenon to the nervous excitement in which I had been living; I feared staying longer, and going mad. “I am going away,” I said to myself. And I raised my arm with a gesture of ill humor and at the same time of decision, looking at the glass; the gesture was there, but scattered, torn, mutilated... I began to dress myself, muttering to myself, coughing without a cough, shaking my clothes with noise, coldly vexing myself over the buttons, so as to say something. From time to time, I furtively looked at the mirror; the image was the same diffusion of lines, the same decomposition of contours... I went on dressing. Suddenly, by an inexplicable inspiration, by an impulse without calculation, it occurred to me... If you are able to guess what my idea was...

- Tell us.

- I was looking at the glass, with the persistence of a desperate man, contemplating my own features spread out and unfinished, a cloud of loose, shapeless lines, when I had the thought... No, you are not able to guess.

- But tell us, tell us.

- It occurred to me to put on the ensign’s uniform. I put it on, made myself completely ready; and, as I was standing in front of the mirror, I raised my eyes, and... I tell you nothing; the glass then reproduced the full figure; not a line missing, not a different contour; it was I myself, the ensign, who finally found the outer soul. That soul, absent with the lady of the estate, dispersed and fled with the slaves, there it was, gathered back in the mirror. Imagine a man who, little by little, emerges from a lethargy, opens his eyes without seeing, then begins to see, distinguishes people from objects, but does not know individually either one or the other; finally, he knows that this is so-and-so, that is so-and-so; here is a chair, there a sofa. Everything returns to what it was before sleep. So it was with me. I looked at the mirror, went from one side to another, drew back, gesticulated, smiled, and the glass expressed everything. I was no longer an automaton, I was an animated being. From then on, I was another. Every day, at a certain hour, I put on the ensign’s uniform, and sat down in front of the mirror, reading, looking, meditating; at the end of two, three hours, I would take it off again. With this regimen I was able to go through six more days of solitude, without feeling them...

When the others came back to themselves, the narrator had gone down the stairs.
